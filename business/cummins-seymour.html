Cummins Seymour Engine Plant

"If it's a high-horsepower Cummins, it was probably built in Seymour." — the unofficial line you'll get from anyone who works at the plant

Category: Manufacturing / Heavy diesel engines · 1900 W County Rd 200 N, Seymour, IN 47274 · Plant founded: 1974

Quick take. Cummins Seymour is one of Cummins Inc.'s flagship engine plants — specifically the home of the QSK and HPCR high-horsepower diesel and natural-gas engines used in mining trucks, locomotives, marine, and stationary power. It's the highest-paying manufacturing job in the area and one of the toughest to get hired into. When people in Seymour say "the engine plant," this is the engine plant they mean.

Who they are

The Seymour Engine Plant opened in 1974 as Cummins' purpose-built site for high-horsepower diesel engines — the K-series and what eventually became the QSK family running in mining haul trucks, locomotives, and marine applications up to 4,400 horsepower. Cummins itself is headquartered 25 miles north in Columbus, Indiana, has been a Fortune 500 company for decades, and is one of the largest U.S.-based engine manufacturers globally. The Columbus campus does design and corporate work; Seymour does the heavy iron.

Cummins as a corporation has a corporate culture distinctive enough that it's been written about in business school case studies — long-tenure CEOs, a deep commitment to community investment in Columbus, vocal corporate stances on diversity, environmental policy, and education. The Seymour plant inherits a lot of that culture even though it's a separate site.

What they do well

Cummins Seymour pays. Production associates start above most other Seymour manufacturers, and skilled trades pay can rival the higher-end private-sector trades work in southern Indiana. Benefits are full-spectrum: medical, dental, vision, generous PTO, real 401(k) match, defined-benefit pension grandfathered for older hires, tuition reimbursement. For Seymour, this is the gold-standard manufacturing job.

The work itself is interesting compared to a typical assembly line. High-horsepower engines aren't built in 60-second takt times — they're large, complex, custom-configured units. A test cell will run an engine for hours. Workers describe genuine engineering depth and problem-solving as part of the work rather than just repetition. For people who like mechanical stuff, this is a draw.

Cummins runs robust apprenticeship and engineering co-op programs in coordination with Purdue and Ivy Tech. Local kids who come up through those programs often turn into long-term Cummins engineers and skilled tradespeople, and those careers stay in the region.

What people complain about

Hard to get hired. The most common complaint isn't from current employees, it's from people who've tried to apply. Cummins Seymour has limited hiring windows and a competitive application pool. Direct hires are rare; most production starts go through staffing-agency contracts (often Aerotek and others) and conversion can be slow.

Cyclicality and layoffs. Mining, locomotive, and marine markets are cyclical. When mining capex contracts, Seymour high-horsepower demand drops, and the plant has gone through layoff cycles — including notable reductions in 2015-2016 and again during the 2020 demand collapse. Indeed reviews flag this. It's not a "guaranteed lifetime employer" — few are anymore, but for a plant that hires hard, the layoff record is part of the picture.

Physical demand and overtime. Heavy engines mean heavy parts. Mandatory overtime during production peaks is documented across review sites. As with Aisin, FLSA-compliant overtime pay applies, but the schedule is the schedule.

How they handle it

Cummins corporate is unusually transparent for a U.S. industrial company. The company publishes detailed sustainability and workforce reports, has a published code of conduct, and historically maintains a high-rated corporate ethics record. Layoff cycles are typically accompanied by severance and outplacement support that exceeds the federal WARN Act minimum. Worker complaints that escalate to the corporate ombuds office tend to be acted on; that's not universal in heavy industry.

Local impact

Seymour plant headcount fluctuates with demand and is generally in the high-1,000s. Combined with Cummins' Columbus operations, the regional employment footprint is enormous — Cummins is the single biggest reason the Columbus-Seymour corridor has the median household income it does. Cummins Foundation grants flow into Jackson and Bartholomew Counties for education, arts, environmental, and equity programs. The company has been an anchor sponsor for STEM education at Seymour Community Schools and contributes to Schneck Medical Center initiatives.

Bottom line

8.5 / 10

If you can get hired at Cummins Seymour, take the job. Pay, benefits, training, and corporate culture are top of the local market. The catch is that getting hired is competitive, the work is physical, and the business is cyclical. As a Seymour neighbor, Cummins (through its Columbus HQ) is the kind of corporate citizen most communities only dream about — sustained, transparent, and locally invested for fifty years and counting.
