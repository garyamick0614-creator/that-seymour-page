Rose Acre Farms

"Good eggs come from happy hens." — the company tagline you'll see on the carton at every Walmart in three states

Category: Agriculture / Egg production · HQ: 6874 N 700 E, Seymour, IN 47274 · Founded: 1939

Quick take. Rose Acre is the second-largest egg producer in the United States and the single biggest agricultural employer in Jackson County. They're privately held, headquartered on a county road north of Seymour, and they have shaped the local economy for three generations. Loved as an employer by long-tenured workers; controversial nationally over a 2018 FDA recall and a multi-year price-fixing case.

Who they are

Rose Acre Farms was founded in 1939 by David Rust as a family egg business in Seymour, Indiana. The company stayed family-owned through the next 80+ years, with Marcus Rust eventually serving as CEO. From a single farm it grew into a vertically integrated operation with 17 production facilities across Indiana, Iowa, Illinois, Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio, and Georgia, and a flock of roughly 25 million laying hens at peak. The corporate office still sits on a quiet stretch of N 700 E north of Seymour, the same county road it's been on for decades — you drive past corn fields, you turn at the sign, and you're at the headquarters of one of the largest egg producers in the country. That's a Seymour story.

The company is private, so financials aren't public, but USDA and trade-press coverage consistently rank Rose Acre as #2 in U.S. shell-egg production behind Cal-Maine. They sell to Walmart, Kroger, Aldi, regional grocers, and food service. The brand on the carton is often "Rose Acre" or "Egg Innovations" depending on line, but a lot of the eggs in Indiana refrigerators came out of a Rose Acre house whether the carton says so or not.

What they do well

Long-tenure employees on Glassdoor and Indeed consistently say two things: the pay is decent for rural Indiana, and the benefits (health, 401k match, paid time off) are real. The company has run profit-sharing in good years. People who stick with Rose Acre tend to stick for a long time — 15, 20, 30 years isn't unusual, and the company makes a thing out of recognizing it. For a privately-held agricultural firm in a region where a lot of farm work is seasonal and uninsured, that's meaningful.

On the operations side, Rose Acre was an early mover into cage-free production well before the major retailer mandates kicked in. They invested heavily in aviary systems and now market a "Heartland Eggs" cage-free line. They were also one of the first to publish animal-welfare reports and submit to United Egg Producers certified-humane audits. Whether those audits go far enough is a separate debate, but compared to peers who never moved at all, Rose Acre moved.

What people complain about

Three recurring themes in public reviews and news coverage:

The 2018 salmonella recall. In April 2018 the FDA forced Rose Acre to recall 207 million eggs from a single facility in Hyde County, North Carolina, after a multi-state salmonella Braenderup outbreak sickened at least 45 people. It was one of the largest egg recalls in U.S. history. The plant was shut down, deep-cleaned, re-inspected, and eventually returned to production, but the episode is permanent in the company's record and any food-safety reporter writing about egg recalls cites it.

Egg price-fixing litigation. Rose Acre was a defendant in the long-running In re Processed Egg Products Antitrust Litigation (MDL 2002) along with Cal-Maine, United Egg Producers, and others. Plaintiffs (food manufacturers including Kraft, Kellogg, General Mills, and Nestlé) alleged a coordinated supply-restriction scheme starting in the 1990s. After a 2023 jury verdict against the defendants, Rose Acre and the others were on the hook for a damages award that ran into the hundreds of millions before trebling. Appeals continued. This is the litigation that anyone Googling "Rose Acre Farms lawsuit" finds first.

Worker complaints on review sites. Indeed and Glassdoor reviews split between long-tenure loyalists and newer/seasonal workers reporting hard physical labor, ammonia exposure in laying houses, and limited promotion paths for line workers. Standard for the industry, but it's there.

How they handle it

On the recall, Rose Acre cooperated with FDA, paid for the recall, and reopened only after the agency cleared the facility. They didn't fight the agency publicly. On the antitrust case they fought hard in court (it's their right to) and lost the jury phase; they're appealing. On worker concerns the company points to its long-tenure rate and benefits package, which is real, even if it doesn't make a 12-hour shift in a layer house easy.

Local impact

Rose Acre is consistently listed in Jackson County's top-10 employers by the Jackson County Industrial Development Corporation. Local headcount estimates run 600-900 between the corporate office and the regional production facilities; the company doesn't break out Seymour-only numbers. They sponsor 4-H projects through Jackson County, support the Jackson County Fair, and have donated to Schneck Medical Center capital campaigns. When something local needs a corporate sponsor, Rose Acre's name often appears on the list.

Bottom line

7 / 10

An old, privately-held, locally-rooted business that punches massively above its weight nationally. As a Seymour neighbor, they pay people, they sponsor stuff, they don't move the HQ to Chicago. As a national food company they have a real food-safety incident on their record and a multi-billion-dollar antitrust verdict on appeal. Both sides are true at the same time.
